# German Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Klammerform (Sentence Bracket & Verb Placement)**: German lyrics frequently place separable prefixes or conjugated auxiliary/modal verbs at the start of a clause while burying the infinitive/participle or separable prefix at the very end of the line (or on the next line). Always assemble the full verbal frame before translating!
  - *Example*: *Ich fange deine Tränen... auf* (*auffangen* = to catch).
- **Subordinate Clause Verb-Final Order**: In clauses introduced by *weil*, *dass*, *wenn*, *als*, *ob*, the conjugated verb sits at the end, often across enjambed lyric lines.
- **Compound Nouns (Komposita)**: German freely fuses concepts into singular compounds (*Herzzerreißend*, *Sehnsuchtsort*, *Gefühlschaos*). Deconstruct the semantic core and render it naturally rather than creating awkward hyphenated calques.
- **Konjunktiv II (Subjunctive)**: Carries deep emotional weight in Lied and musical lyrics, denoting longing, unfulfilled desire, or wistful regret (*wärst du doch hier*, *ich könnte fliegen*).

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- A separable verb prefix waiting on the following line (*Ich gebe diesen Kampf nicht... / auf*).
- An auxiliary verb (*habe*, *bin*, *wird*) with its past participle or infinitive on the next line.
- A subordinate connective (*obwohl*, *während*, *damit*) spanning multiple rhythmic bars.
- Relative clauses introduced by *der/die/das/dessen/denen*.

## Common Traps

- Never translate line-by-line when a separable verb or auxiliary bracket spans multiple lines.
- Do not let heavy consonant clusters (*str*, *pf*, *cht*) mislead you into thinking the song is harsh; German lyric poetry often conveys profound vulnerability and tenderness (*Zärtlichkeit*).
- Distinguish formal *Sie* (rare in pop lyrics, present in theatrical/satirical pieces) from familiar *du* / *ihr*.
