# Spanish Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Sinalefa (Metrical Synalepha)**: In Spanish song lyrics, when a word ends with a vowel and the next word begins with a vowel (or silent *h*), they fuse into a **single spoken/sung syllable**. A line written with 12 graphic words may only have 7 or 8 sung beats.
- **Subject Ellipsis**: Spanish is a pro-drop language; conjugated verbs encode person and number (*tengo*, *sabes*, *quiere*). Explicit pronouns (*yo*, *tú*, *ella*) are inserted only for dramatic emphasis or contrast.
- **Enclitic Pronoun Binding**: Pronouns attach to infinitives, gerunds, and imperatives (*diciéndotelo*, *mírame*, *olvidarme*).
- **Double Negation**: Standard Spanish grammar uses double negatives (*no quiero nada*, *nunca he visto a nadie*); these are intensifications, not positive logic.
- **Regional Variants**: Distinguish Iberian Spanish (*vosotros*, distinction /θ/) from Latin American Spanish (*ustedes*, seseo, regional urban slang like *parce*, *chaval*, *morra*).

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- A prepositional phrase or subordinate conjunction (*aunque*, *para que*, *mientras*, *cuando*) completed on the next line.
- An enjambment splitting a verb from its direct object or gerund phrase (*sigo / pensando en ti*).
- Conditional constructs (*si tú me miras... / yo me vuelvo loco*).
- Anaphora and repeated parallel clauses typical of reggaeton or flamenco coplas.

## Common Traps

- Do not interpret double negation as a mathematical positive.
- Beware of false cognates with English or Portuguese (*éxito* = hit/success, not exit; *embarazada* = pregnant, not embarrassed; *actualmente* = currently, not actually).
- Do not invent explicit pronouns where Spanish relies on the dramatic intimacy of verb endings.
