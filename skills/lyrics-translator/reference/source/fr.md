# French Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Phonetics vs. Spelling**: Spoken/sung French differs significantly from written text due to liaison (linking final silent consonants to following vowels), elision (dropping weak vowels), and enchaînement.
- **The "E Caduc" (Mute E)**: In traditional chanson and musical theatre, final unstressed *-e* is often vocalized as an extra musical note/syllable (e.g., *le vent-e*), whereas in contemporary pop and hip-hop it is routinely dropped.
- **Slang and Verlan**: Modern French lyrics heavily feature colloquial slang (*argot*), youth expressions, and inverted verlan (*meuf* for *femme*, *chelou* for *louche*, *zarbi* for *bizarre*). Translate their semantic intent, not their phonetics.
- **Register & Distance**: Observe *tu* (informal/intimate) vs. *vous* (formal/plural/courteous).

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- Relative clauses beginning with *qui*, *que*, *dont*, *où* that extend across lines.
- Pronoun clitic chains (*il me l'a dit*, *je m'en vais*) completed by a verb on the next line.
- Prepositional phrases (*afin de*, *au milieu de*, *avant que*) resolved on subsequent lines.
- Subjunctive or conditional clauses (*si j'avais su...*, *bien que tu sois...*) completed later.

## Common Traps

- Do not mistake verlan or colloquial contractions (*t'as* = *tu as*, *j'suis* = *je suis*, *y'a* = *il y a*) for errors.
- Beware of faux amis when translating to English or Romance languages (*actuellement* = currently, not actually; *sensible* = sensitive, not sensible).
- Avoid translating poetic French imagery with rigid prose; capture the melancholic, romantic, or cynical nuance (*la mélancolie*, *le spleen*).
