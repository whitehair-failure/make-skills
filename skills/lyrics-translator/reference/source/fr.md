# French Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Phonetics vs. Spelling**: Spoken/sung French differs significantly from written text due to liaison (linking final silent consonants to following vowels), elision (dropping weak vowels), and enchaînement.
- **The "E Caduc" (Mute E)**: In traditional chanson and musical theatre, final unstressed _-e_ is often vocalized as an extra musical note/syllable (e.g., _le vent-e_), whereas in contemporary pop and hip-hop it is routinely dropped.
- **Slang and Verlan**: Modern French lyrics heavily feature colloquial slang (_argot_), youth expressions, and inverted verlan (_meuf_ for _femme_, _chelou_ for _louche_, _zarbi_ for _bizarre_). Translate their semantic intent, not their phonetics.
- **Register & Distance**: Observe _tu_ (informal/intimate) vs. _vous_ (formal/plural/courteous).

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- Relative clauses beginning with _qui_, _que_, _dont_, _où_ that extend across lines.
- Pronoun clitic chains (_il me l'a dit_, _je m'en vais_) completed by a verb on the next line.
- Prepositional phrases (_afin de_, _au milieu de_, _avant que_) resolved on subsequent lines.
- Subjunctive or conditional clauses (_si j'avais su..._, _bien que tu sois..._) completed later.

## Common Traps

- Do not mistake verlan or colloquial contractions (_t'as_ = _tu as_, _j'suis_ = _je suis_, _y'a_ = _il y a_) for errors.
- Beware of faux amis when translating to English or Romance languages (_actuellement_ = currently, not actually; _sensible_ = sensitive, not sensible).
- Avoid translating poetic French imagery with rigid prose; capture the melancholic, romantic, or cynical nuance (_la mélancolie_, _le spleen_).
