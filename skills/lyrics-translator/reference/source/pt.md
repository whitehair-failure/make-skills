# Portuguese Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Brazilian vs. European Portuguese**:
  - Brazilian Portuguese (PT-BR): Dominates MPB, Bossa Nova, Samba, Funk, and Sertanejo. Uses _você/vocês_, proclitic pronouns (_me dá um beijo_), and progressive gerunds (_estou cantando_).
  - European Portuguese (PT-PT): Fado and Portuguese rock. Uses _tu/vós_, enclitic pronouns (_dá-me um beijo_), and infinitive aspect (_estou a cantar_).
- **Nasal Vowels & Diphthongs**: Sounds like _-ão_, _-ões_, _-ã_, _-em_ form the phonetic soul of Portuguese lyrical rhyming and emotional cadences.
- **Untranslatable Cultural Concepts**: Words like _saudade_ (deep, bittersweet nostalgic longing) or _cafuné_ (tenderly running fingers through hair) cannot be reduced to single shallow words; render their emotional resonance in context.
- **Syncopation & Bossa Nova Meter**: Portuguese lyrics often break conversational prose into syncopated, whispering syllables aligned with guitar chords.

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- Progressive aspect or compound tense split across lines (_fiquei / pensando_).
- Relative clauses introduced by _que_, _quem_, _cujo_, _onde_.
- Conditional or concession clauses (_se você vier..._, _mesmo que o mundo acabe..._).
- Parallel refrains typical of samba-enredo or lyrical fado.

## Common Traps

- Never conflate Brazilian colloquial grammar with European grammatical standards; keep the song in its intended regional soul.
- Beware of false friends between Portuguese and Spanish/English (_puxar_ = to pull, not to push; _presunto_ = ham, not presumed; _esquisito_ = weird/strange in Portuguese, but exquisite in Spanish).
- Avoid overly cerebral translations of raw street funk or breezy beach Bossa Nova.
