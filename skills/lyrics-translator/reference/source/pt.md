# Portuguese Source Lyrics

## Read Before Translating

- **Brazilian vs. European Portuguese**:
  - Brazilian Portuguese (PT-BR): Dominates MPB, Bossa Nova, Samba, Funk, and Sertanejo. Uses *você/vocês*, proclitic pronouns (*me dá um beijo*), and progressive gerunds (*estou cantando*).
  - European Portuguese (PT-PT): Fado and Portuguese rock. Uses *tu/vós*, enclitic pronouns (*dá-me um beijo*), and infinitive aspect (*estou a cantar*).
- **Nasal Vowels & Diphthongs**: Sounds like *-ão*, *-ões*, *-ã*, *-em* form the phonetic soul of Portuguese lyrical rhyming and emotional cadences.
- **Untranslatable Cultural Concepts**: Words like *saudade* (deep, bittersweet nostalgic longing) or *cafuné* (tenderly running fingers through hair) cannot be reduced to single shallow words; render their emotional resonance in context.
- **Syncopation & Bossa Nova Meter**: Portuguese lyrics often break conversational prose into syncopated, whispering syllables aligned with guitar chords.

## Semantic-Chunking Signals

Make one semantic block when consecutive lines contain:

- Progressive aspect or compound tense split across lines (*fiquei / pensando*).
- Relative clauses introduced by *que*, *quem*, *cujo*, *onde*.
- Conditional or concession clauses (*se você vier...*, *mesmo que o mundo acabe...*).
- Parallel refrains typical of samba-enredo or lyrical fado.

## Common Traps

- Never conflate Brazilian colloquial grammar with European grammatical standards; keep the song in its intended regional soul.
- Beware of false friends between Portuguese and Spanish/English (*puxar* = to pull, not to push; *presunto* = ham, not presumed; *esquisito* = weird/strange in Portuguese, but exquisite in Spanish).
- Avoid overly cerebral translations of raw street funk or breezy beach Bossa Nova.
